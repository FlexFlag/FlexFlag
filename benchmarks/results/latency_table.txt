Analysis
=========
NOTE: These are wall-clock measurements including time.Now()/time.Since() overhead
(~50-100ns on macOS). The absolute ns values should be interpreted as relative
comparisons, not raw lock costs.

Scenario   | RWMutex p50 | RWMutex p99 | RWMutex p999 | Snapshot p50 | Snapshot p99 | Snapshot p999
-----------+-------------+-------------+--------------+--------------+--------------+--------------
none       |   512ns     |  1024ns     |   2048ns     |    64ns      |   128ns      |   128ns
steady     |   512ns     |  1024ns     |   2048ns     |    64ns      |   128ns      |   256ns
burst      |   512ns     |  1024ns     |   2048ns     |    64ns      |   128ns      |   256ns
highwrite  |   128ns*    | 16384ns     | 131072ns     |    64ns      |   128ns      |   256ns

*RWMutex p50 improves under highwrite because reads that DO run complete faster
 when writer holds the lock briefly (less contention on the read path itself);
 the degradation is visible at tail latencies (p99/p999).

Answer to "World (a) or (b)?"
==============================
World (b) at ≤100 writes/sec: RWMutex does NOT degrade. p99 and p999 are
identical across none/steady/burst scenarios. The write rate is too low to
meaningfully block reader goroutines.

World (a) emerges at continuous (saturated) write rate (highwrite scenario):
  - RWMutex p99 spikes from 1024ns → 16384ns (16x increase)
  - RWMutex p999 spikes from 2048ns → 131072ns (64x increase)
  - Snapshot p99/p999 remain flat at 128-256ns regardless of write pressure

The degradation threshold is between 100 writes/sec (no effect) and
saturated continuous writes (~tens of thousands/sec). In a real flag system,
flag updates are measured in writes/hour, so RWMutex tail latency degradation
is unlikely in production — but the atomic snapshot approach is immune by design
and demonstrates the principle clearly.
